
Your friends may not be “disgusting” simply because they have sexual thoughts or desires. Sexual desire is a normal part of human development; what matters psychologically is whether they act respectfully, safely, consensually, and within their own values.

## Desire vs. behavior

Having a “biological need” is a loose way of saying they feel sexual arousal, curiosity, or a desire for intimacy. Those feelings can be influenced by puberty, hormones, attraction, loneliness, stress, boredom, and social media—but a feeling is not an instruction to act. [psycnet.apa](https://psycnet.apa.org/manuscript/2023-28034-001.pdf)

Psychology separates **desire** from **decision-making**. Someone can feel a strong urge and still choose not to act on it, delay it, or handle it privately. Your friends may be struggling with impulse control, peer pressure, curiosity, or wanting approval—not necessarily being bad people. [psycnet.apa](https://psycnet.apa.org/manuscript/2023-28034-001.pdf)

## Why they act that way

- **Peer influence:** People often behave more sexually when they believe their friends are doing it, approve of it, or expect it. Perceived peer behavior can matter even more than direct pressure. [courses.lumenlearning](https://courses.lumenlearning.com/adolescent/chapter/sexual-development/)
- **Immediate reward:** Sexual activity, flirting, pornography, or crude jokes can give quick excitement or relief, while consequences such as embarrassment, pregnancy risk, STIs, or guilt feel distant. [psycnet.apa](https://psycnet.apa.org/manuscript/2023-28034-001.pdf)
- **Identity and belonging:** Some people use sexual talk or behavior to feel mature, accepted, powerful, or “normal” among friends.
- **Poor boundaries:** They may not yet have learned how to communicate clearly, respect a “no,” or recognize that consent must be ongoing and enthusiastic. [college.mayo](https://college.mayo.edu/media/mccms/content-assets/about/college-profile/consumer-information-and-disclosures/VAWA-v1.pdf)
- **Different values:** You may value restraint, privacy, respect, religion, or emotional readiness more than they do. That difference does not automatically make either side evil—but it can make their behavior feel alienating to you.

## What is not okay

It is reasonable to feel uncomfortable if they pressure others, brag about non-consensual things, share private images, mock people, or try to make you join in. Sexual behavior should never involve pressure, force, manipulation, or ignoring someone’s boundaries; consent means a clear, voluntary yes, and it can be withdrawn at any time. [college.mayo](https://college.mayo.edu/media/mccms/content-assets/about/college-profile/consumer-information-and-disclosures/VAWA-v1.pdf)

## What you can do

You do not have to participate, laugh along, or accept their standards. You can say plainly:

- “I’m not comfortable with this topic.”
- “Don’t involve me in that.”
- “That’s not funny to me.”
- “If someone says no or seems unsure, stop.”

If they are pressuring someone, sharing intimate material without consent, or crossing clear boundaries, distance yourself from that behavior and tell a trusted adult or appropriate authority if anyone may be at risk. Sexual health also includes protecting against STIs and pregnancy when people are sexually active, but safety never replaces consent. [mayoclinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/sexually-transmitted-diseases-stds/symptoms-causes/syc-20351240)

The psychological point is: **their urges may be normal; reckless, disrespectful, or coercive behavior is not.** You can understand the impulse without approving of the way they handle it.
