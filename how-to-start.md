Your friend probably isn’t choosing to waste time because they don’t care. More likely, knowing what to do and **starting** it are two different skills—especially when the task feels overwhelming, scary, boring, or emotionally loaded.

## Why “just do it” is hard

When someone avoids an important task, the avoidance often gives short-term relief: no risk of failing, being judged, making the wrong choice, or feeling incompetent. But that relief reinforces the avoidance, so the pattern repeats.

Doomscrolling can be part of that loop. It offers constant novelty and distraction, while the brain is naturally drawn to threatening or negative information; social-media feeds are designed to keep supplying it without a natural stopping point.  People may also scroll because they feel anxious or helpless and want a sense of control—even though the scrolling usually makes them feel worse. ## What may be happening

| What it looks like | Possible internal experience |
|---|---|
| “They know exactly what to do but do nothing” | The first step feels too big, vague, or emotionally expensive |
| They keep scrolling instead | Their brain is seeking immediate relief from discomfort, boredom, or anxiety  [health.clevelandclinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/everything-you-need-to-know-about-doomscrolling-and-how-to-avoid-it) |
| They make a “bad move” | They may be reacting to pressure, panic, shame, or exhaustion rather than thinking clearly |
| They seem unmotivated | They may be stuck in avoidance, low mood, burnout, ADHD-style executive-function struggles, or fear of failure |

“Knowing what to do” is often intellectual knowledge. Actually doing it requires initiating action, tolerating uncertainty, breaking the task into steps, and managing emotions—none of which are automatic.

## How to help without shaming

Avoid saying, “Why don’t you just do it?” That can increase shame, which usually makes avoidance stronger.

Try something more practical:

- “What is the smallest first step you could do in the next 10 minutes?”
- “Do you want company while you start, or do you need space?”
- “What part feels hardest: starting, deciding, or finishing?”
- “Let’s make the task ridiculously small—open the document, send one message, put on shoes.”
- “After 15 minutes, we can stop. You do not have to solve everything today.”

A useful reframe is: **they do not need to feel motivated first; they need to make the first action easy enough to survive.** Setting a short time limit and replacing scrolling with a concrete activity are commonly recommended ways to interrupt the loop. [mcpress.mayoclinic](https://mcpress.mayoclinic.org/mental-health/doom-scrolling-and-mental-health/)

## When to be concerned

If this is persistent and paired with deep sadness, hopelessness, major sleep/appetite changes, inability to function, panic, or talk of self-harm, encourage them to talk with a mental-health professional or trusted adult. Doomscrolling can coexist with anxiety and depression, though it does not by itself prove either condition. [psychologytoday](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/screen-time/202502/doomscrolling-why-cant-we-stop)

You can care about them without carrying responsibility for fixing them. A good message might be: “I can see you’re stuck, and I’m not judging you. Want to do one tiny step together, or tell me what would actually help right now?”
